# Week 1 Presenter Notes

Use the [talk outline](talk-outline.md) for the full agenda and teaching prompts. These notes provide language for framing and transitions; adapt them to the actual guest presentations.

## Opening (10m)
“Today you will see three people without traditional software-engineering backgrounds show useful things they have built with AI. Pay attention to their process as much as their finished product. What did they try first? What went wrong? What did they change about how they worked?”

“This residency assumes you can already code, run a project, and use Git. We will build on that foundation so you can deliver software with AI and explain why someone should trust your work. An impressive demo is a starting point for questions about engineering, not the end of those questions.”

Put four headings on screen: **Built, Method, Turning point, Evidence**. Ask students to take notes under them. Explain that they will choose one observed method to try at home.

Briefly cover GitHub-only materials, bring-your-own paid agent access in VS Code and terminal, and 5–10 hours of weekly independent work. There is no project build in today's class; Week 2 starts a fresh guided build.

## Introduce and Connect the Speakers (75m Total)
Introduce each guest through their problem and background. Do not invent details or rank the guests by how technical their products appear.

After the first presentation, note the early method and the turning point. Between speakers, connect one concrete observation while leaving room for differences: “We just saw how a smaller first version helped get the project moving. As you watch the next example, notice how they chose what to build first.” Use that transition only if the first presentation actually supports it.

If a guest says “I just kept prompting,” ask: “Can you show an example of what you changed in your request?” If they say “It worked,” ask: “What did you do to check that?” Keep these interruptions short and preserve the panel time.

## Facilitate Q&A (25m)
Begin with a comparison drawn from the presentations, then prioritize student questions. Invite speakers to explain a specific incident rather than offer only general advice.

If the discussion becomes a list of favorite tools, bring it back to decisions: “What did that tool let you do differently, and how did you check the result?” If it becomes too technical for the purpose of this session, capture the question for Week 2 or Week 3 and return to the builder's experience.

Close by asking each guest for one practice students can test this week and one trap to watch for.

## Synthesis (5m)
“Here are the patterns we actually saw today.” Select two or three from your notes and point to the guest examples behind them. Do not claim there is one universally correct workflow.

“Write down one method you want to try and one question you still have. Next week we'll use your first attempts to understand context, requests, and validation. Later we'll move to sustained agents and then coordinated workflows.”

## Homework Briefing (5m)
Open [homework.md](homework.md) and show the deliverables. “Verify your setup, try building or changing something in your own repository, and report honestly. If it fails, show what you tried and what you learned. You do not need to arrive next week with a polished product.”

Remind students that Week 2 is a fresh project. Ask them to locate the assignment and identify the repository or PR link they will submit. Record any access blockers for follow-up.
